Abstract

Clinical AI is largely evaluated on retrospective data, which may not capture sufficient clinical and anatomical variability. Synthetic imaging could address this limitation but requires clinical fidelity and precise control. We developed a counterfactual computed tomography (CT) framework using two 3D diffusion models trained on 12,367 CT pulmonary angiograms for pulmonary embolism (PE) and 8,444 head CTs for intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). Each counterfactual kept the anatomy of a real patient and added a lesion. We generated 2,347 PE and 2,601 ICH counterfactuals across 300 patients. Radiologist review confirmed their realism and anatomical consistency. Stress-testing two detectors per disease exposed operating limits not revealed by aggregate metrics: overall sensitivity was 62-67% (PE) and 59-72% (ICH) but decreased to 42-48% for PE under 0.5 mL and 30-56% for ICH under 5 mL. Detection also varied with hemorrhage subtype and, in one PE detector, the presence of pleural effusion. The volume-dependent sensitivity computed on counterfactuals transferred to four external real-world datasets. Counterfactuals can reveal clinically relevant features that influence model performance. We open-source our code, models, and synthetic cohorts.

Method

A three-dimensional latent diffusion model generates each counterfactual from two conditioning signals: a segmentation mask that constrains it to a patient-specific anatomy, and a radiology report impression edited to describe the inserted finding while preserving every other finding. Holding the anatomy segmentation fixed and changing only the pathology mask yields matched volumes that share the anatomy segmentation but carry different disease.

Pulmonary embolism. Emboli are placed within the pulmonary arterial tree under vascular constraints, varying anatomical location (central to subsegmental), degree of occlusion (partial to complete) and clot burden (isolated to extensive bilateral), for roughly 16 configurations per patient. Every candidate passes a four-check automated validation.

Intracranial hemorrhage. Hemorrhage masks selected from real patient cases span hemorrhage volume, all five major subtypes (epidural, subdural, subarachnoid, intraventricular, intraparenchymal), single- and multi-compartment bleeds, and intra-axial and extra-axial locations.

The generators extend MAISI, fine-tuned on 12,367 CT pulmonary angiography volumes (INSPECT) for PE and on 8,444 non-contrast head CT volumes for ICH.

Are the counterfactuals realistic?

Two radiologists reviewed 520 scans: 430 disease-positive counterfactuals, 60 disease-negative synthetic scans, and 30 real scans. Anatomical quality was rated acceptable (score ≥ 1) in 93% of PE counterfactuals (177 of 190) and 98% of ICH counterfactuals (236 of 240), and overall realism in 91% (173 of 190) and 96% (231 of 240). Within-patient resemblance was strong to near-identical for 73% of PE patients (22 of 30) and 97% of ICH patients (29 of 30).

Blinded to the detectors' output, the radiologists confirmed the presence of PE in 63% of PE counterfactuals (119 of 190) and detected ICH in 74% of ICH counterfactuals (177 of 240). Fréchet Inception Distance against matched real test scans was 1.29 (95% CI 1.26–1.33) for PE and 0.242 (95% CI 0.237–0.265) for ICH.

Where do the detectors fail?

We stress-tested two detectors per disease on the counterfactuals, comparing their predictions with the intended lesion mask. Overall PE sensitivity was 67% (PE-Detector-1) and 62% (PE-Detector-2). Sensitivity increased with embolus volume, from 48% and 42% below 0.5 mL to 95% and 93% above 5 mL. Only central emboli exceeded 90% detection (93% and 91%); subsegmental emboli were detected in 46% and 39% of cases.

For ICH, overall sensitivity was 59% (ICH-Detector-1) and 72% (ICH-Detector-2), increasing from 30% and 56% below 5 mL to 95% and 90% above 50 mL. Detection varied by subtype, from 45% for intraparenchymal to 78% for intraventricular hemorrhage for ICH-Detector-1.

Lesion properties associated with detection

In multivariable logistic models, lesion volume showed the strongest association with detection for all four detectors: per standard deviation increase in log volume, the odds of detection were 1.84 and 2.06 times higher for the PE detectors and 3.02 and 1.56 times higher for the ICH detectors. For PE, clot burden was the only other property associated with detection, and only for PE-Detector-1; location, severity, occlusion and embolus shape were not. For ICH, subtype and the number of co-occurring subtypes were also associated with detection. Pleural effusion was the only co-occurring finding associated with lower PE detection, and only for PE-Detector-2 (odds ratio 0.50, 95% CI 0.36–0.69).

Do the synthetic operating limits hold on real scans?

We evaluated the PE detectors on three external, radiologist-annotated real CTPA datasets (FUMPE, CAD-PE and READ). For emboli of at least 2.5 mL, sensitivity on real scans was 99% to 100%, compared with 94% and 92% on the synthetic scans. For emboli under 0.5 mL, sensitivity on real scans was 71% to 80% for PE-Detector-1 and 58% to 74% for PE-Detector-2, compared with 48% and 42% on the synthetic scans.

On PhysioNet CT-ICH (36 real non-contrast head CTs), real detection met or exceeded the synthetic estimate in every volume bin for both ICH detectors. The synthetic estimates were therefore conservative, so operating limits identified on counterfactuals are unlikely to overstate real-world performance.
